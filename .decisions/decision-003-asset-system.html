Option A
Chosen

Curated Drawing Library

Every pose × angle × person is a separate drawing. 20 people × 5 angles × 30 actions = 3,000 drawings.
Every character, pose and angle is a finished hand-drawn SVG. Start from Open Peeps (free to reuse, already mix-and-match, close to Marker Comp) and commission the rest. The quality is the highest and the most human-looking. But the library can only show what someone has drawn, and your list (many angles, zoom levels, actions, plus driving) multiplies into thousands of drawings.

Works well when

  • You want a v1 in weeks with a small but beautiful set
  • A handful of hero poses covers most journeys

Watch out for

  • Open Peeps has no side, back or over-the-shoulder views and no driving pose
  • The agent keeps asking for poses that don't exist and falls back awkwardly
  • Adding people means redrawing every pose

Option B
Chosen

Primitive Pose Rig

Figures are capsules on a skeleton. Every pose is free, but everyone looks like the same mannequin.
Characters are simple capsules on a 2D skeleton (the teal dots are joints). The agent writes "pose": "driving", the rig bends the joints, and the marker renderer draws it. Any pose, any angle, instantly, and the editor can let people drag a hand. But everyone is a mannequin: no hair, no clothes, no age, no body diversity. Your item 1, a diverse cast, basically disappears.

Works well when

  • Coverage matters more than character
  • You want to prototype the whole pipeline in days

Watch out for

  • Mannequins undercut empathy, and seeing a real person is the whole point
  • Diversity is limited to colors and sizes
  • Looks like a 3D-app placeholder, not a storyboard

Option C
Chosen

AI-Generated Panels

About $0.003–0.04 and 1–60 s per panel. Characters drift between panels, and nothing inside the picture can be dragged.
The coding agent (or the tool) calls an image model per panel with a Marker Comp style prompt. It's the widest range possible: any scene, any person, any camera, including awkward real-life moments. But each panel is a flat picture, so nothing inside it can be moved, swapped or re-posed, which breaks the editor. Maya also changes face and clothes between panels, and each render takes seconds and costs money.

Works well when

  • You only need one-off illustrations, not an editor
  • Unusual scenes (hospital, farm, factory) matter more than consistency

Watch out for

  • Character drift is the #1 reason AI storyboards fail in production
  • No drag, resize or swap inside the image, which breaks the core loop
  • Needs an API key, costs money per panel, and needs a network connection

Option D
My pickLeanChosen

Modular Parts on a Rig

~40 parts per angle × 4 angles, and the rig poses them. 12 people × any pose, all editable.
Hand-drawn parts (heads, hair, torsos and outfits, arms, legs), each drawn at 4 angles and snapped to the same 2D skeleton as B. You get B's unlimited poses with A's human, hand-drawn character. Diversity comes from mixing parts (skin tone, age, body shape, hijab, wheelchair, cane, hair), and Open Peeps parts are a free starting point for the front view. The agent writes { who: "maya", pose: "driving", angle: "side" } and everything stays editable.

Works well when

  • You want both a diverse cast and endless angles and poses
  • Characters must stay identical across 12 panels
  • It needs to run offline and free, with no AI API calls

Watch out for

  • The biggest art investment: roughly 40 parts × 4 angles, plus fixing joints so elbows don't look broken
  • Rigged 2D figures can look stiff if poses aren't hand-tuned
  • Front views ship first; side and back views take longer
Respond with: "Option D"

Scenes & devices, which are the same in every option

These are simpler and don't need a rig, so they're plain hand-drawn SVG libraries with metadata:

  • Scenes (home/kitchen, office/desk, car/driver-seat, coffee-shop/counter…) are drawn in layers (back wall, mid props, foreground) with named marks such as counter-front or driver-seat, so the agent can say "Maya at the counter" instead of giving coordinates. Camera shots (wide, medium, close, over-the-shoulder) crop and scale the layers.
  • Devices (phone, laptop, tablet, watch, car dashboard, TV, kiosk…) each ship with a screen rectangle plus perspective corners, so uploaded designs snap onto the screen automatically (decision 7), and with grip points, so a hand on the rig can hold them.
  • Bubbles, captions, time cards, taps, cursors and "wiggle" motion marks are drawn by code in the marker style, with no art assets needed.

The case against my pick

D is the most expensive option and the one most likely to look bad before it looks good. 2D rigs are notorious for broken-looking elbows, floaty hands and stiff poses, and there's no mature open-source rig built for storyboards, so you'd be building one. A (a curated library) ships something beautiful in weeks. A small set of well-drawn front and three-quarter poses might cover 70% of real storyboards, and you could grow into D later. A realistic path is to start with A's art, cut into D's parts from day one, so the rig can be switched on later without redrawing anything.

This recommendation assumes

  • A diverse cast and the full range of angles and poses are both must-haves, not nice-to-haves
  • The tool must work offline and free once installed, since it's CLI-first and open source
  • Someone (you, a commissioned illustrator, or contributors) can draw about 160 parts in the Marker Comp style

What the research says

  • Open Peeps is free to reuse (CC0) and already mix-and-match (hair, face, body/pose, about 585k combinations), but poses come only as bust, about 30 standing and about 18 sitting, with no side or back views. Open Peeps
  • The licenses of unDraw, Storyset and Blush don't allow redistributing their art as a pack or using it in integrations, which rules them out for an open-source tool. license reviews Blush license
  • Storyboarder (the open-source film tool) solved poses and angles with a posable figure ("male kneeling medium looking up"). Users draw over it, which shows the rig approach works for boards. Wonder Unit Shot Generator
  • Browser tools already attach SVG artwork to skeletons (Pose Animator, skeleton-rig), so "parts on bones" is a known technique. TensorFlow Pose Animator
  • Character drift is described as "the single biggest reason generic AI image tools cannot be used for production storyboarding". Agencies now use AI for reference only. ToonyStory Seb Antoniou
  • Image generation costs about $0.039 per image (Gemini Flash Image) and $0.011–0.167 (GPT-image). Latency ranges from under a second to 60+ seconds. Google OpenAI forum

Where people disagree

Some storyboard teams are moving to AI for speed and variety and accept drift. Others say editable, consistent characters are the product. What decides it for you: your core loop is "agent drafts, human tweaks in an editor". Only vectors support that loop, so AI can at most be an optional extra later (e.g. "generate a new scene background").
