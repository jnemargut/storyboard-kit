The classic film-storyboard look: pencil on toothy paper, hatching for shadows, and faint non-photo-blue construction lines left in, the way real board artists work. Only the product moments (the order screen, the tap) pick up a red pencil. It looks like a draft that invites argument, which is ideal for early discovery crits.

Works well when

  • You want boards to feel unmistakably unfinished, so people critique the journey instead of the drawings
  • Teams already know film and animation storyboards

Watch out for

  • Low contrast gets muddy when shrunk into slides or Slack
  • Hatching on many parts can make scenes busy

Marker Comp

Permanent Marker (titles) · Patrick Hand (lettering) · Work Sans (editor)
The UX and industrial-design storyboard staple: a black fineliner outline over 3 cool-grey marker tones that sit slightly off the line, like real markers. The rule that carries it: the software is the only thing in color. Every screen, tap and notification is teal, and everything else in the person's life is grey. Anyone glancing at a board sees at once how small and how late the product shows up in someone's day.

Works well when

  • Boards get shared in decks, Slack and printouts (high contrast survives shrinking)
  • You want the 'where does our product actually show up?' insight to jump out
  • Flat fills are the easiest style to generate consistently from modular parts

Watch out for

  • Can drift toward 'finished illustration' if the line wobble is tuned too clean
  • Less emotional range than ink and wash

Gaegu (lettering) · Nunito (editor)
The hand-drawn diagram look tech teams already know from whiteboard tools: doubled wobbly lines, diagonal hachure fills in bright pastels, and friendly lettering. It's the cheapest to build, because open-source libraries (rough.js) produce this look from any shape. The downside is that people will read it as a diagram, not as a person's life.

Works well when

  • The storyboard should sit comfortably next to existing architecture diagrams
  • You want the fastest possible path to a working renderer

Watch out for

  • Looks like every other whiteboard sketch, so there's little identity
  • People drawn in this style feel stiff and robotic, which undercuts the empathy goal
  • Pastel rainbow fills fight the 'product is the highlight' idea

Comic-book energy: brush-ink outlines, loose watercolor washes that bleed past the lines, halftone dots and ALL-CAPS comic lettering. It's the most emotional of the four, so frustration, relief and exhaustion read clearly on faces and in the mood of a scene. It also looks the most crafted, which is exactly the risk.

Works well when

  • Emotion is the point, e.g. journey maps about stressful moments (healthcare, money, travel)
  • Boards are shown to execs and need to feel compelling

Watch out for

  • Reads as higher fidelity, so teams polish the boards instead of questioning the journey
  • Washes are the hardest to keep consistent across panels
  • Color washes compete with the product highlight

The case against my pick

You said low fidelity twice, and Graphite (A) is lower-fi than Marker. Pencil thumbnails signal "this is a draft, rip it apart" more loudly than anything else here. Marker comps are what agencies use when a board needs to impress, and that pull toward polish is how storyboards stop being thinking tools and turn into deliverables. If your users mainly storyboard in early discovery with their own team, A is probably the better tool. There's also a hybrid worth considering: B's "only the product is in color" rule applied to A's pencil.

This recommendation assumes

  • Boards travel: they get pasted into decks, Slack threads and research readouts, where contrast at small sizes matters
  • The core insight you want boards to deliver is "where does the software show up in this person's day?", and a single product color makes that visible
  • Characters are built from modular parts (decision 3), which suits flat fills better than hatching or washes

Why these four

  • They span the real storyboard traditions: film pre-visualization (graphite), design and ID comps (marker), the tech whiteboard (rough), and comics (ink and wash).
  • The decision research found that open-source sketch libraries (rough.js, perfect-freehand) and SVG turbulence filters can produce A, B and C live in the browser while keeping every stroke editable. D's washes are the hardest to generate. rough.js SVG pencil effect
  • The Open Peeps library (free to reuse, hand-drawn line art) is the closest ready-made match for styles A and B, which is useful for decision 3. Open Peeps

Where practitioners disagree

One school says rougher is better: polish shuts down critique, so keep boards ugly. The other says boards have to travel and persuade stakeholders who weren't in the room, so legibility beats roughness. What decides it: are these boards mostly for the team's own thinking (A), or mostly for convincing people outside the team (B)?
