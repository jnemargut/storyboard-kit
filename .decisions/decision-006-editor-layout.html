The familiar design-tool layout: an asset library on the left (cast, scenes, devices, bubbles, gestures), the board in the middle, and a property inspector on the right showing the selected panel's shot, focus, cast, screen and gesture. Every field in the inspector maps one-to-one onto the JSON, so it doubles as a way to learn the vocabulary. It's powerful and discoverable, but it's a lot of chrome, and it pulls attention toward settings rather than the story.

Works well when

  • Designers know this layout from Figma
  • Everything is findable, and fields mirror the JSON exactly

Watch out for

  • The board gets about half the screen
  • Encourages fiddling with properties instead of thinking about the journey
  • Cramped on a laptop

One panel fills the stage and the whole sequence runs along a filmstrip at the bottom, like film storyboard tools and Keynote. It's great for crafting a single frame, with a big canvas for posing Maya's arm or placing a tap exactly. But you only ever see one moment in detail, and storyboards are about the flow between moments, which the filmstrip shows at thumbnail size.

Works well when

  • The most room for detailed posing and screen placement
  • Familiar from Storyboarder and slide tools

Watch out for

  • Loses the whole-journey view where service-design insight happens
  • What you edit isn't what you export (the export is a page, not a slide)

The board is the editor: the exportable page sits in the middle and you click a panel to edit it right there. A small dark toolbar appears above the selection (shot, swap cast, add bubble, screen), + Add opens the asset drawer only when you need it, and a layout menu switches between 2×2, 3×2, a 1×6 strip and so on. What you see is exactly what exports, and the whole journey is always visible.

Works well when

  • What you see is what you export
  • The whole journey stays visible while you edit, which is the point of the tool
  • Minimal chrome, so it fits the low-fi, sketchbook feel

Watch out for

  • Detailed posing happens at panel size (needs zoom-to-panel)
  • Contextual toolbars hide features until you select something

Panels run left to right, with service-design lanes underneath: a Feeling curve, a Product row marking which moments involve the software (teal), and a Workaround row for sticky notes on what people really do. It's the most direct expression of your thesis: the empty product squares and the dipping feeling curve make the messiness of real life impossible to ignore. But it turns a storyboard into half a journey map, which is more to fill in and more to explain.

Works well when

  • Forces the service-design questions: where are we absent, and where does it hurt?
  • The agent can fill the lanes from the prose too
  • A differentiated artifact no comic maker has

Watch out for

  • More than you asked for, and a heavier first draft
  • Wide horizontal layout exports awkwardly to slides
  • The lanes add input effort to every panel

The case against my pick

Your whole reason for building this is to make people think like service designers, and D is the only layout that actively makes them. C is a well-made comic editor, and people could use it to draw a happy-path marketing story without noticing where Maya's life gets messy. The lanes in D turn the gaps into something visible on screen: an empty product square, a dip in the curve, a workaround sticky note. If you'd rather the tool push the thinking than just allow it, choose D, or C with D's lanes as a toggle ("Show journey lanes") that also exports.

This recommendation assumes

  • The exported image is the storyboard page itself, and people want to edit exactly that
  • The agent does most of the heavy composition, so the editor is mostly for tweaks, not building from scratch
  • Designers mostly work on laptops, where two side panels squeeze the board
